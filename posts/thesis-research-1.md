## What is Quantum Mechanics

In order to understand my work, we must start with quantum mechanics. You’ve probably heard of it. You may even know some of its famous predictions! I’d be surprised if you haven’t come across Schrödinger’s eponymous cat, both dead and alive at the same time.

Fundamentally, quantum mechanics says that there’s a smallest amount of matter (stuff) and energy, and that all of the matter we observe is made up of these discrete, smallest bits. The idea sounds simple, because it is! However, it leads to a whole lot of weird consequences. Let’s discuss a few of those consequences now:

1. Particles and waves: when we quantize (make quantum) a physical system, it turns out that we have to assign particle like and wave like properties to, well, everything. Things we think of as particles (like atoms) behave in ways that are wave like, and things we think of as waves (like light) behave like particles. In this way, things like atoms can interfere, similarly to the way ripples on the surface of a lake interfere to create interesting patterns when two rocks are dropped nearby each other.
2. Quantum states: a second strange result of quantization is that particles can only exist in discrete energy states. (I hear you purists saying “what about continuous quantum numbers?”, to which I say, “please don’t be pedantic, you’ve made it this far already!”) The full evolution of a quantum system is determined by which states a given system can “interfere with” or access. This leads to the third point I want to mention, which is
3. Probabilistic interpretation: quantum mechanics is not deterministic! We can mathematically describe the probability distribution of finding a quantum system in a given state, but when we go to measure the system, we will observe only one of many possible outcomes. This is another way of describing that famous cat attached to that German name: a quantum system can be made up of multiple quantum states (cat is alive, cat is dead, etc.) at once. We call this the superposition principle. If we reset the system (and we must reset it, if we do nothing, the following is _not_ true), and the perform the measurement again, we may observe a different quantum state. What we can know is the distribution of how likely we are to observe any given experimental result.

Armed with these three facts, I’m ready to de-jargonify part of my thesis title. “Matter wave quantum (emitters)” just means we have atoms, and as we set them and connect them to… something jargony… we can observe matter, which we now know can be wave-like, as waves.
