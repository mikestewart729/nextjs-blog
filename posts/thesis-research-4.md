## Studying Quantum Phenomena

I want to highlight that the description I am going to give you here describes only _one_ way that we can study quantum mechanics. You've likely heard of some other ways (for example, studying high energy physics at the Large Hadron Collider at CERN in Geneva), but those techniques give access to different kinds of physics than those I am interested in describing to you here. 

You might ask why, if the world is quantum mechanical, we don't really see quantum behavior in our daily lives. The answer to this puzzle is one of "size". Matter becomes quantum mechanical only at very small scales. We're talking "electron" sized or so, in physicist's terms, nano-meter scale. A nanometer (nm) is 0.000000001 meters. That's so small, things of this size become really difficult to observe. We can imagine that a quantum system even 1000 times bigger (on the micrometer (μm) scale) would become accessible to current microscopy (or other) techniques, and this is indeed the case! So let's first talk about how we can force a quantum system to grow to be 1000 times the usual size.

## Bose-Einstein Condensates

I mentioned that quantum systems exist in specific states. If I can put a really large number of identical particles (here, think atoms) in the same state, the "size" of that state would in some sense grow to become large. It turns out that this is possible! First theorized by Bose and expanded upon by Einstein, there is a quantum state of matter (which is not a solid, liquid, or gas as we understand from high-school chemistry) where the matter becomes quantum mechanical, but thousands of times larger than things usually display quantum properties. We call this state of matter a Bose-Einstein Condensate, or BEC for short. (My New Yorkers may have different connotations for BEC, and while I suggest that you DO pop on in to the bodega to get yourself a snack now, BEC in this text will refer to Bose-Einstein Condensates.) Because all of the atoms are in the same quantum state, anything we do to the system becomes much easier to observe, using for example lasers and CCD cameras, due to the "extra size" of the system. BECs only exist at really cold temperatures since energy and temperature are intimately related, and to get millions of particles in the lowest energy state, we need cold temperatures. These temperatures are so cold that physicists cleverly refer to them as "ultracold". How cold? BECs happen when you reduce millions of identical atoms down to 100 nano-Kelvin. I can't really describe this temperature in human terms, but let me invoke absolute zero. BECs form at 0, a decimal point, seven zeroes, and then a 1 of a degree above absolute zero. So, a BEC exists at ultracold temperatures, but how can we make one in the laboratory?

## Making BECs

How on **earth** do we cool something down to 100 nK? Nitrogen becomes liquid at around 77 Kelvin, so that's too hot to use as a coolant. You might even think to use liquid helium, but helium becomes liquid at around 4 Kelvin, so again, that's much too hot for what we need. It turns out that the answer to this puzzle is as counter-intuitive as possible: laser cooling!

Surely you're scratching your head a bit, since when you go out into the sun, you warm up. Lasers are light as well, so wouldn't shining a laser at something also heat it up? This is indeed true, except that laser light is unique, and quantum mechanics comes to the rescue. The special fact we need about laser light is that it comes in exactly one color (frequency and color are essentially the same thing), whereas most light that we're used to (such as sunlight) consists of many colors at once. The quantum mechanical fact that we need is that, in some sense, atoms are picky about color. An atom has many different quantum mechanical energy levels, and if the difference in energy between two such levels matches a color, an atom can absorb light of that color. The atom will never absorb light of another color. Thus, by choosing an atom and a laser color pair carefully, we can use the following analogy. 

Firstly, how quickly something moves is directly related to its temperature. The slower something moves, the colder it is. Therefore, imagine that an atom is like a bowling ball rolling down the bowling alley. This bowling ball can absorb green laser light, which we can imagine as a gun of green ping-pong balls that you're holding at the other end of the bowling alley, where the pins usually are. Since you don't wan to get hit by the bowling ball, you want to use your laser ping-pong ball gun to slow down the atom. One ping pong ball won't slow the bowling ball down very much, but a concentrated, consistent stream of ping pong balls (which is incidentally almost how lasers work) can slow down the bowling ball. This is the central idea of laser cooling, if I'm allowed to brush the finer details under the rug! I can slow down an atom by shining laser light at it, as long as I carefully choose the color of the light. If I use such a laser in 6 directions, right, left, front, back, top, and bottom, I can slow down the bowling ball/atom no matter which way it moves. Using a magnetic field as a bottle, I can capture many of these slowed down atoms, and in so-doing, reduce their temperature to about 100 micro-Kelvin. 

That's a huge improvement over our liquid Nitrogen/Helium idea from before, but it's not yet cold enough, so we will need to do something more. This time, we do something that instead is very intuitive. Much like your morning coffee when it is too hot for you to drink, we let the hottest atoms from our cold collection _evaporate_. The hottest atoms leave our magnetic bottle, since they have the energy to escape, and then if we wait long enough, the remaining atoms reach a new equilibrium at a colder temperature, just like your coffee. In practice, we force this process to occur in the lab with radio-frequency waves forcing higher energy atoms out of our bottle, but the idea is the same as the simple case of evaporation-cooling that you're used to in your daily life. In this way, we lose atoms, but we reduce the temperature the remaining way to the 100 nano-Kelvin that we need. If we do this with enough atoms, we reach BEC! 

Recall, we wanted a BEC because it behaves quantum mechanically, but we can see it much more easily. It turns out that BECs will be a useful platform for building both quantum emitters and engineered reservoirs, and we create both of these using so-called optical lattices, which may sound more impressive when I tell you that optical lattice is just physicist-speak for "crystal made of light".

## Optical Lattices for building Quantum Emitters and Engineered Reservoirs

We've used laser light in its "particle like" manifestation in our ping-pong ball analogy above. Let's return to our usual understanding of laser light as a wave now. If I shine a laser against a mirror and send it back upon itself, I can create a standing wave. A standing wave is just a wave which doesn't move, that is, its crests (bright spots) and troughs (dark spots) do not move in space or time. Since the atoms in our BEC like to be in certain light, they will tend to be pushed towards the bright spots or dark spots in this standing wave, and in this way, we can effectively create a crystal made out of light. By changing the color slightly, we can change whether the atoms are attracted to the bright spots or dark spots in our light crystal, or the spacing of the atoms in the crystal. We can even make the light crystal "invisible" to certain atoms! These properties allow us to change the interaction of atoms with the background of states, and in this way, we have created engineered reservoirs. Lastly, we create quantum emitters by viewing the individual wells of an optical lattice as excited (high energy) when there is an atom trapped in the well, and as ground-state (low energy) when unoccupied. By using specially chosen microwave radiation, we can flip atoms/matter-waves into or out of these wells, and in this way, we create arrays of quantum emitters of matter-waves for experiments.
