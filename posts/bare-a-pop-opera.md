# We've never been this bare

Jason, sorry, Romeo dies at the end. Spoiler warning I suppose for a 26 year old musical and, I suppose, an at least 429 year old play that most of us will have read at some point. I promise your knowing this fact will in no way diminish the experience of *Bare: A Pop Opera*, a small musical with a cult following that feels to me like a strangely appropriate mirror for this moment. In an age when Catholicism, writ-large, is on-trend, this reimagining of the Shakespearean tragedy of Romeo and Juliet's star-crossed lovers as closeted Catholic School boys asks us to ponder how faith, authenticity, and dogma shape our lives. Even now, having left my Catholic upbringing behind nearly two decades ago, I find myself returning to this intimate, rock-inflected score, these relatable characters, and the image I see of who I once was. *Bare* dares its audience to be the kindest version of themselves, through its book and score, and challenges us all to embrace what makes us similar, rather than what divides us. Lest we "unlearn the truth \[you] shared with us", the time has never been more appropriate for a revival of *Bare: A Pop Opera*.

# The Best Kept Secret

*Bare: A Pop Opera* is in many ways one of the best kept secrets in the world of musical theater. Originally premiering in 2000 in Los Angeles, *Bare* is a sung-through musical with music by the late Damon Intrabartolo and lyrics by Jon Hartmere. It tells the story of five friends in their final semester at St. Cecilia's, a private, Catholic boarding school, not too different from my own (though admittedly, mine was not a boarding school). The main characters are Peter, a closeted boy on the verge of coming out, Jason, the most popular boy in class and Peter's secret boyfriend, Nadia, Jason's twin sister and self-described "Plain Jane Fat-ass", Ivy, the often pigeonholed as too-pretty-for-depth popular girl, and Matt, "always a bridesmaid, never the bride" to Jason's number one status academically. Throughout *Bare*, we learn the hopes, dreams, fears, and mistakes of these five, along with a supporting cast of their friends and teachers, as the book deftly weaves a frame-narrative of a class production of Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet*. The students stumble, fall, and fly through a tale of star-crossed lovers, and the classic tragedy serves as a leitmotif for Peter and Jason. 

*Bare: A Pop Opera* started as a tune in Damon Intrabartolo's head, or so he claims in *Navigate This Maze - Bare: A Pop Opera Documentary* produced by (DETAILS HERE, CITATION). Jon Hartmere immediately contradicts him, claiming that the show began lyrics first, at least in its core numbers, in a retelling reminiscent of the Marlon James novel *Black Leopard, Red Wolf* (CITE), wherein almost every statement of fact is immediately contradicted by another character in the retelling. (MIKE: What are you doing here besides showing off that you read? Like, why bring Black Leopard, Red Wolf into the conversation? Because it's also queer and you enjoyed it?) Regardless of which version of events is true, the result was an operatic, sung-through story in the pop and rock musical idioms. The result was something groundbreaking. *Bare* premiered in Los Angeles at the Hudson Theater, running for just over four months (CITE), and by all accounts, it was a smash success. After four years, *Bare* premiered off-Broadway at the American Theatre of Actors, running for just six weeks before closing (CITE). Rather than being relegated to musical theater history, the creative team created a two-disc studio recording of the show, and this recording continues to introduce new generations of theater fans to this hidden gem of a story. Despite its short run, *Bare* has been revived off broadway, in Los Angeles, in black-box theaters across the United States, and even translated and produced around the world in the two decades since its creation. The music and story resonate for so many, and continue to do so, in part due to the compassion with which the show depicts its characters and the sincerity and heart of its score. 

# Argumenti Capita

I would be remiss in talking about *Bare* if I were to not tell you the events of the story. I'll do my best to do so, and to keep it compelling, but your best bet may be to try to find any number of recordings online from any of the various productions. *Bare* is a show best understood by experiencing its magic firsthand. If you are averse to theater recordings, be they pro-shot or bootleg (which, I would never encourage), the full cast album is available on most music streaming platforms, and I can't recommend it enough. If you are already familiar with *Bare*, or don't feel like reading a plot summary, skip to the next section.

But I digress. *Bare: A Pop Opera* opens on a dream-sequence, or rather a nightmare during Catholic mass, an experience I know all-too-well, which shows Peter visions of shame and damnation (including a delightfully ribald cock-amamie pun in the form of a Catholic hymn) due to his hidden homosexuality (Epiphany). After the mass, Peter runs into Jason in the hallway, where we learn of their clandestine relationship, and Peter suggests that Jason try out for the St. Cecilia's production of *Romeo and Juliet* (You & I). Jason initially rejects the idea and leaves Peter alone to contemplate what he really desires from his life and his relationship with Jason (Role of a Lifetime; note: some productions move this number into Act II). Jason changes his mind and auditions for the play, where the director, Sister Chantelle, casts him as Romeo alongside Ivy's Juliet (Auditions). Disappointed about being cast as the nurse, Jason's twin sister Nadia laments, "why can't the fat girl play Juliet? It's the plumpest of roles, so then why not pick me?" (Plain Jane Fatass). The classmates plan a trip to a rave (Wonderland), but Nadia, feeling isolated and realizing her humor is no defense at a rave, decides not to attend (A Quiet Night at Home). At the rave (Rolling), Peter and Jason dance together, but conscious of prying eyes, Jason leaves the venue. Peter expresses his desire to come out, but Jason insists that they keep hiding (Best Kept Secret), after which they share a kiss, which Matt accidentally sees. The next morning, Peter and Matt seek guidance from the church's teachings (Confession). During rehearsals, Ivy's talent for illustration is discovered, and after Nadia jokes about her sluttiness, Ivy reflects on the life that she feels is being written for her, while Matt expresses his attraction to her (Portrait of a Girl). Matt plans and throws a birthday party for Ivy (Birthday, Bitch!), during which Ivy rejects Matt and pursues Jason (One Kiss). Having unknowingly eaten pot-brownies, a stoned Peter and Matt share a moment lamenting being ignored at Ivy's party (Are you there?). An innocent question about who usually leads when dancing leads Peter to confide a secret to Matt, who flees the scene in shock. Peter is then visited by the virgin Mary and told that since his secret is out, he owes it to his mother to bell her himself (911! Emergency!). During rehearsals the next day, tensions run high and Matt calls Jason a "faggot" (Reputation Stain'd). After Sister Chantelle tells the students to disperse, Peter tells Jason about his vision and his plan to come out to his mom, but Jason tells him that he can't come out for fear of what his own parents would do (Ever After). Nadia practices the cello and sings a humorous song about her hatred of springtime (Spring). Ivy apologizes to Jason for her behavior at the party, but insists she wants more. While Matt pines for Ivy and Nadia longs to be noticed, Peter calls someone seeking comfort and receives no answer. Ivy and Jason sleep together (One), and the curtain falls on act I. 

When the curtain rises on act II, we find our characters at Peter and Jason's wedding, officiated by Sister Chantelle. Ivy cuts in as Jason's bride, which Peter's mom Claire celebrates, and then Peter awakens from the nightmare (Wedding Bells). After a brief, cold encounter between Peter and Jason (In the Hallway), Ivy professes that her feelings for Jason are the most real she's ever felt, only for Jason to realize he made a mistake (Touch My Soul). Peter calls Claire and attempts to come out, only to have Claire dodge all of his attempts (See Me). After the call, Claire reflects on how she feels inadequately prepared to support her son in his moment of honesty (Warning). Ivy misses rehersals, and after her understudy forgets her lines, Peter steps in and performs the scene and dances with Jason in front of the cast (Pilgrim's Hands). After the students disperse, Peter returns to the rehearsal space to retrieve his bag, and Sister Chantelle, who has "been around enough priests to hear \[him] loud, proud, and clear", tells Peter that he's exactly how he was meant to be (God Don't Make No Trash). Nadia confronts Ivy about missing rehearsals, and Ivy, reflecting on how their friendship fell apart, informs Nadia that she's pregnant (All Grown Up). Later, in the rehearsal hall, Jason is practicing his commencement address, when Ivy confronts him and tells him that he knocked her up. Ever-hopeful Ivy hopes that maybe this event will bring her and Jason closer together, but Matt enters and accuses Jason of playing with Ivy's feelings because he's gay. Nadia and Peter arrive, and when Jason tries to deny Matt's accusations, Peter confesses that he told Matt about Jason after Ivy's birthday party (Promise). Jason, feeling out of control reflects on his relationship with Peter (Once Upon a Time), and seeks clarity from the church. Unable to provide assurance to the struggling youth, the priest tells Jason he must reject his sexuality to remain within God's love (Cross). Backstage before the play, Jason receives pills from the "pharmacist", a fellow student, Lucas. Nadia attempts to comfort Ivy, and Jason proposes that he and Peter flee St. Cecilia's, which Peter refuses, since he no longer wishes to hide or fear himself (Two Households). As the play starts, Jason takes the drugs and then holds Peter back. The two share a tender, heartbreaking moment of memory for their past relationship as well as parting in the irreconcilable present, both promising that they've loved each other from the start (Bare). During the play, Jason becomes feverish and collapses on the stage (Queen Mab). As Jason dies, the cast of Romeo and Juliet sing the end of Romeo and Juliet (A Glooming Peace). Peter confronts the priest about the compassion he was unable to give Jason, and ultimately Peter forgives the priest in the face of traged (Absolution). The show ends with Matt delivering the graduation valediction, while the students reflect on the roles they played in Jason's death and attempt to face an uncertain future (No Voice). 

# Do you remember the day that you met me?

I first discovered *Bare* in 2009. While the exact moment I discovered the show is lost to the vagaries of my fallible memory, one detail stands out: the first song I heard from the show was Peter's desperate attempt to come out to his mom in "See Me". I immediately latched on to the anguish of trying to come out to someone important to you, and the all encompassing fear of rejection. I, too, was in the middle of my own coming out journey. My freshman year at the University of Michigan was eye-opening in many ways: I had never attended a non-Catholic school before, I was living for the first time away from my parents, and, most importantly, my floor in South Quad had three neighboring rooms with out, queer students living in them. Gone were my days of cautiously coming out to one friend at a time; I quickly came out and began to live my life openly, going so far as to become involved heavily in the undergraduate LGBT commission. The affirmation I found at school reminds me of the affirmation Peter found in the words of Sister Chantelle. Though I had broken with the Catholic Church of my childhood, due in no small part to finding church catechism about homosexuality irreconcilable with my own life, I found a community of my own whose support allowed me to flourish. 

It wasn't all flowers and sunshine. This was the era of "Don't Ask, Don't Tell", the Defense of Marriage Act, and the successful passage of Proposition 8 (banning gay marriage) in California. Queer representation was beginning to become mainstream, and not only as a punchline, but it was a far-cry from today, where we are able to seek and experience queer storytelling across all facets of life. It is in this context that *Bare* was born, and dare-I-say, came of age. 

Peter was a beacon of hope for me. He found strength in himself to live his truth, and through my own growing community of queer friends and allies, I too found this strength. When I eventually asked my own mother to "See Me", I wasn't prepared for the outright negation of my identity that I initially received. Looking back, I could have handled the conversation differently, but by way of defense, I was 19 years old, and what 19 year old has everything figured out? During the uncomfortable years that followed, and in an attempt to repair the broken relationship, I attended a meeting of a support group for Catholic ex-gays and their family members. In this regard, I felt my life following Jason's leitmotif in Cross; my being gay wasn't ok and never would be. Thankfully, I was not forced to leave the supportive environment at school, and at the cost of attending online meetings of this group (and later, telling my parents I was attending without actually attending), I was able to begin moving the needle about continuing to live my life openly. Thankfully, *Bare* (and anotehr musical I may write about sometime, *Next to Normal*) was there for me, providing a light and support in the quite moments when I wasn't surrounded by friends or community. If Peter could live his truth and in his own strength, so could I. 

# Hear My Voice

Why is it that this small little musical resonated so strongly with me and with others? I believe *Bare*'s enduring power in part stems from the universality of its characters. Who amongst us hasn't felt scared, lost, or alone at some point? Which of us, especially as teenagers, hasn't found ourselves woefully out of depth in the face of adult problems? But I also believe that it runs deeper than a reflection of our deepest selves on an intimate stage. In order to understand *Bare*, we must examine the score and lyrics. While I can speak to the lyrics and melodies that I find most affecting, most enduring, I turn to the work [Inside BARE, background and analysis](https://newlinetheatre.com/barechapter.html) by Scott Miller to help explain the music theory. (This may have later been published in *Sex, Drugs, Rock & Roll, and Musicals*, try to get a copy at The Drama Bookshop). 

*Bare*, being operatic in style (if not musical genres), is full of leitmotifs representing ideas and themes. A leitmotif is a short, recurring musical phrase associated with a specific character or idea, and interestingly, while each character gets a soliloquy or aria, most leitmotifs in *Bare* represent themes of the work. It is unsurprising then that the first melodic idea we hear is the *bare leitmotif*. This simple tune will recur throughout the musical, highlighting moments of emotional clarity and truth, moments where the characters are made bare, even though we don't hear the melody in context until late in act II. The *bare leitmotif* sublimates the emotional throughlines of the show into a simple but impactful melody, and by teasing our ears with the theme throughout the show, we are ready to accept Peter and Jason's moment of clarity when the leitmotif finally has its moment to shine. This technique of utilizing the central thematic melody of the show first, before any other music can be heard, is used to stunning effect in Disney's *Hunchback of Notre Dame*, both the film and stage musical (as discussed in the *excellent* YouTube video [Why Hellfire is the Best Disney Villain Song Ever](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-dJLcqHdRv8)). There, the first melody we hear is called "Olim", a Latin translation of the song "Someday". While cut from the film version and relegated to the end credits, it was originally intended as Esmeralda's song upon seeking sanctuary in the eponymous cathedral (a role filled on both stage and screen by "God Help the Outcasts"). "Someday" speaks to Esmeralda's desire for a world free of prejudice and greed, where her Romani people are not persecuted by nature of their birth, a theme which echoes and serves as a foil to the diverging journeys Quasimodo and Judge Claude Frollo travel through this adaptation. Hearing "Olim" and the *bare leitmotif* at the outset prepares our ears as an audience to pay attention when we hear the melodies in full. This technique serves as a brilliant signpost, telling us "hey, you, this part is really important". We'll return to the *bare* scene shortly.
