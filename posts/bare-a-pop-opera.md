# We've never been this bare

Jason, sorry, Romeo dies at the end. Spoiler warning I suppose for a 26 year old musical and, I suppose, an at least 429 year old play that most of us will have read at some point. I promise your knowing this fact will in no way diminish the experience of *Bare: A Pop Opera*, a small musical with a cult following that feels to me like a strangely appropriate mirror for this moment. In an age when Catholicism, writ-large, is on-trend, this reimagining of the Shakespearean tragedy of Romeo and Juliet's star-crossed lovers as closeted Catholic School boys asks us to ponder how faith, authenticity, and dogma shape our lives. Even now, having left my Catholic upbringing behind nearly two decades ago, I find myself returning to this intimate, rock-inflected score, these relatable characters, and the image I see of who I once was. *Bare* dares its audience to be the kindest version of themselves, through its book and score, and challenges us all to embrace what makes us similar, rather than what divides us. "How did \[we] unlearn \[this] truth you shared with \[us]?"

# The Best Kept Secret

*Bare: A Pop Opera* is in many ways one of the best kept secrets in the world of musical theater. Originally premiering in 2000 in Los Angeles, *Bare* is a sung-through musical with music by Damon Intrabartolo and lyrics by John Hartmere. It tells the story of Peter and Jason, two seniors at St. Cecilia's, a private, Catholic boarding school, as well as the stories of several of their classmates. Through the frame story of St. Cecilia's production of Romeo and Juliet, we come to learn the hopes, dreams, and fears of the students, and we join in their messy, underinformed explorations of sexuality, drugs, and coming-of-age. *Bare* had a short off-broadway run in 2004, the same year that I started attending a private Catholic high school, though before I had come to terms with my own queerness; this theme in the show oddly echoed my own life when I discovered a recording of a production from somewhere in (if memory serves) Indiana in 2009. This was the era of "Don't ask, Don't Tell", the Defense of Marriage Act, and Proposition 8's successful passage in California. To my baby-gay eyes, Peter and Jason served as both beacons of hope and as a cautionary tale. 

It is into this relatively culturally homophobic era that *Bare* entered the scene. True, *Will and Grace* had begun airing, and other queer characters were beginning to appear in film, tv, and theater, but it was the tale of two Catholic school boys tragically falling in love which resonated with me, in no small part due to the empathy with which it was told. In (very) brief summary, *Bare* opens with Peter experiencing a nightmare of his damnation due to his sexuality, consistent with Catholic catechism at the time, kicking off a series of events wherein he comes to know his own strength and decides to come out.
